When people start believing they can find the truth for themselves, some of them find some very new truths.

Take 1848. Hydesville, New York. Right in the Burned-Over District. Two young sisters, Maggie and Kate Fox, tell their family they're hearing knocking sounds in the house. And the knocks answer questions. Once for yes. Silence for no. They say it's a spirit.

Word spreads. The family moves to Rochester. The girls start holding séances, and people pay to attend. The famous showman P.T. Barnum even puts them on display in New York City. And a whole movement is born: Spiritualism, the belief that the dead can talk to the living.

Think about what happens about a dozen years later. The Civil War kills hundreds of thousands of young men. Millions of families are grieving. So Spiritualism explodes. Even Mary Todd Lincoln held séances in the White House after her son died.

Oh, and the knocking? In 1888, Maggie Fox admitted on stage that it had been a trick. She'd been cracking her toe joints.

New churches were popping up too. The Unitarians taught that Jesus was a great teacher, but not God. The Universalists taught that everyone would eventually be saved. And in 1830, a young man from the Burned-Over District named Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon, which he said he translated from golden plates. His followers were chased from New York to Ohio to Missouri to Illinois, where a mob killed Smith in 1844. His successor, Brigham Young, led them all the way to Utah.

Religious freedom was in the Constitution. But when people actually used it in new ways, a lot of Americans got very uncomfortable. And nothing made them more uncomfortable than the newcomers stepping off the boats.
