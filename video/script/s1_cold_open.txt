December, 1829. Savannah, Georgia.

A ship pulls into port from Boston. Somewhere on board are sixty copies of a small pamphlet. Not guns. Not gunpowder. A pamphlet.

They end up with a local Black preacher. He reads a few pages, and he immediately hands them to the police.

Within weeks, the mayor of Savannah is writing panicked letters to the mayor of Boston. Georgia starts passing new laws. And a rumor spreads across the South that there's a price on the author's head. Thousands of dollars. Dead or alive.

So what was in this thing? Who wrote it? And why did a few pages of paper scare an entire state?

We'll get there. I promise. But to understand why that pamphlet hit like a bomb, we have to zoom out. Because in the 1830s and 40s, America was going through something really strange.

The whole country caught a fever. Not a real one. A fever to fix things. Drinking. Prisons. Schools. Marriage. Religion. Women's rights. Slavery. Everybody had a cause, and everybody was sure theirs would save the nation.

So here's the question I want to answer today: Why did an entire country, all at once, decide it needed to be fixed?

And the answer starts with a tent.
