Some reformers didn't want to fix one problem. They wanted to rethink life itself.

In New England, a group of writers called the Transcendentalists said you shouldn't just follow what church or government or society tells you. Look inward. Look at nature. Trust yourself. Ralph Waldo Emerson put it in an essay called "Self-Reliance."

His friend Henry David Thoreau actually tried it. He built a tiny cabin at Walden Pond and lived there for two years to see what life looked like stripped down to the basics. Quick myth-bust, though: Walden was only about a mile and a half from town, and Thoreau walked in all the time, sometimes for dinner at his family's house. Not exactly the wilderness.

But Thoreau also spent a night in jail for refusing to pay a tax that he said supported slavery and an unjust war. He wrote about it in an essay called "Civil Disobedience," and a century later it inspired Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

Others went further and built entirely new societies. We call these utopian communities.

The Shakers, led by a woman they called Mother Ann Lee, shared all their property and lived by strict rules. They got their name from their worship services, where they'd shake and dance to cast off sin. They also believed in total celibacy. No marriage. No kids. They grew only by winning converts and taking in orphans. Which worked for a while, peaking around 6,000 members. But as of 2025, there were exactly three Shakers left.

Then there's Oneida, in New York, founded in 1848 by John Humphrey Noyes, a man who'd been converted by, you guessed it, Charles Finney. Noyes believed his followers had already become perfect. So private property had to go. And he decided marriage was a kind of private property, too. At Oneida, nobody was allowed an exclusive relationship. Later, a committee even decided which members were allowed to have children.

The community stayed financially successful for decades making silverware, a company you can still buy from today. But Noyes and a few leaders held enormous power over members' lives, including teenagers, and much of what happened there was abusive. In 1879, facing outside pressure and possible criminal charges, Noyes fled to Canada, and the experiment fell apart.
