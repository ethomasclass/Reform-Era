In 1841, a schoolteacher named Dorothea Dix volunteered to teach a Sunday school class at a jail outside Boston.

What she found there changed her life. Locked in with the prisoners were people with mental illnesses. They hadn't committed any crime. They were just sick, and nobody knew what else to do with them. Some were in cells with no heat, in the middle of a Massachusetts winter.

So Dix did something nobody expected. She started traveling. Town to town, jail to jail, poorhouse to poorhouse, writing down everything she saw. Calmly. In detail.

Then she took her notes to the state legislature. Remember, she couldn't vote, and she couldn't hold office. But she could write. And she told those lawmakers the mentally ill of their state were being kept "in cages, closets, cellars, stalls, pens! Chained, naked, beaten with rods, and lashed into obedience."

Imagine being a politician and hearing that read out loud about your own state.

It worked. Dix did this over and over, state after state, and helped found or expand more than 30 hospitals. One woman with a notebook changed how an entire country treated people with mental illness.
