Between 1815 and 1860, about five million immigrants poured into the United States. Most came from two places: Ireland and Germany.

The Germans often arrived with a little money and bought farmland in places like Pennsylvania and Ohio. The Irish usually arrived with nothing. In 1845, a disease wiped out Ireland's potato crop, the food most poor Irish families lived on. Around a million people starved or died of disease. More than a million fled, and a huge number of them landed in cities like Boston and New York, taking the lowest-paying, most dangerous jobs they could find.

And a lot of them were Catholic. In a country that was overwhelmingly Protestant, that set off an alarm.

This hostility toward immigrants has a name: nativism. And it got ugly fast.

In 1834, a mob outside Boston burned a Catholic convent to the ground. Two years later, a book hit the shelves called Awful Disclosures of Maria Monk. It claimed to be the true story of a young woman who escaped a convent in Montreal, and it accused priests and nuns of shocking secret crimes.

It was a sensation. It became one of the best-selling books in America before the Civil War.

There was just one problem. It was fake. Investigators found nothing. Maria Monk's own mother said her daughter had never even lived in the convent. The book had been shaped by anti-Catholic Protestant ministers who knew exactly what fears they were selling.

Sound familiar? A shocking story that confirms what people already fear spreads faster than the truth does. That was true in 1836, and it's true on your phone today.

By the 1850s, nativists had their own political party. Members were told to keep it secret, so when outsiders asked about it, they'd answer, "I know nothing." So everybody called them the Know-Nothings. And for a few years, they won real elections, including governors and nearly the entire Massachusetts legislature.

So not every reformer was trying to include more people. Some were trying to keep people out. Hold onto that too.
