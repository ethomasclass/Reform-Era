Notice something about Dorothea Dix? She was doing all this without being able to vote.

In fact, once a woman got married in the 1800s, the law basically erased her. Under a rule called coverture, she couldn't own property, keep her own paycheck, sign a contract, or sue in court. Legally, she and her husband were one person. And that person was him.

Now, a lot of the most active reformers were women. They organized temperance societies, raised money, and gave speeches against slavery. And eventually a few of them looked around and asked: wait, we're fighting for everyone's freedom but our own?

In 1840, Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott traveled to London for a big anti-slavery convention. When they got there, the men voted not to seat them. They had to sit behind a curtain and watch. At a convention about freedom.

They were furious. And eight years later, in 1848, they held their own convention in Seneca Falls, New York. And yep, Seneca Falls is also in the Burned-Over District.

Stanton wrote a document on purpose to sound like the Declaration of Independence, with one little change: "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal."

Then came the most controversial demand of all: the right to vote. Even some people in the room thought that went too far. It passed, barely, after the formerly enslaved abolitionist Frederick Douglass stood up and argued for it.

Women wouldn't win the vote nationally for another 72 years. Of the 100 people who signed at Seneca Falls, only one, Charlotte Woodward, was still alive in 1920 when it finally happened.
