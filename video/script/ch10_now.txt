Which brings us back to that pamphlet in Savannah. Because the biggest reform fight of all was about slavery.

First, you need to know that lots of white Americans in the early 1800s said they were against slavery. But most of them wanted to end it slowly. This is called gradual emancipation. The Northern states had done it this way. Pennsylvania's 1780 law didn't free a single enslaved person that day. It only freed children born after the law, and only once they turned 28.

Others backed an idea called colonization: free enslaved people, then ship them to Africa. The American Colonization Society even founded a colony, Liberia, for this. Presidents supported it. But most free Black Americans hated it. Their families had lived here for generations. This was their country.

And that's where David Walker comes in. Walker was a free Black man who ran a used-clothing shop near the Boston docks. In 1829, he published his Appeal. It said slavery had to end, not someday, but now. It called out white Christians and Thomas Jefferson himself for their hypocrisy. And it said enslaved people had every right to resist. He wrote, "America is more our country, than it is the whites, we have enriched it with our blood and tears."

Then he got creative. He sewed copies into the linings of coats he sold to sailors heading south. That's how sixty copies ended up in Savannah.

The South panicked. Georgia passed new laws against spreading anti-slavery writing, and rumors spread of a bounty on Walker: $10,000 alive, $1,000 dead. In 1830, Walker was found dead in Boston. It was most likely tuberculosis, but plenty of people never believed that.

And one of the people Walker influenced was a young white printer named William Lloyd Garrison.

Here's what's interesting. Garrison actually started out supporting gradual emancipation and colonization. Then he spent seven weeks in a Baltimore jail for calling out a shipowner who was trading enslaved people. He listened to Black abolitionists. He read Walker. And he completely changed his mind.

On January 1, 1831, he launched a newspaper in Boston called The Liberator. And on page one, he went straight at the gradualists. Asking him to be moderate about slavery, he wrote, was like telling "a man whose house is on fire, to give a moderate alarm."

Then he ended with this: "I am in earnest. I will not equivocate. I will not excuse. I will not retreat a single inch. And I will be heard."

Garrison demanded immediate emancipation. No waiting. No paying slaveholders. No shipping anyone to Africa. Early on, most of his paying subscribers were free Black Americans.

Seven months later, Nat Turner led a bloody rebellion of enslaved people in Virginia, and white Southerners blamed Garrison. That November, the Georgia legislature offered $5,000 to anyone who would arrest him and bring him to Georgia for trial.

And the North didn't love him either. In 1835, a mob in Boston dragged Garrison through the streets with a rope, and the mayor locked him in jail just to keep him alive. Years later, he publicly burned a copy of the Constitution, calling it "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," because it protected slavery.

Most Americans, North and South, thought Garrison was a dangerous extremist. But he never backed down.
